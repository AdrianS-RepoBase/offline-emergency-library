Emergency Radio Communications Guide — Germany

Funk im Notfall: CB, PMR446, Freenet, Amateurfunk, Rundfunk-Empfang (Deutschland)

How to communicate and stay informed by radio when phones and internet are down, under German rules (Bundesnetzagentur, BNetzA). German terms are given in italics — handy with the offline dictionary in this kit (Deutschland\Woerterbuch).

Rules in normal times: CB, PMR446 and Freenet are license-free (Allgemeinzuteilung, no registration, no fees) but only with type-approved equipment (CE mark) and no modification of power or antenna. Amateur radio needs a license + call sign from the BNetzA (exam). Receiving broadcasts is always fine. Listening to authority/rescue radio (BOS, Behördenfunk) or commercial traffic is not allowed. In a genuine life-threatening emergency, using any available radio to call for help can be justified (rechtfertigender Notstand, § 34 StGB) — no guarantee, so learn and practice before you need it. This guide is not legal advice; rules change — check bundesnetzagentur.de when online.

0. First: official emergency channels (not radio)

What How
Fire / rescue / ambulance 112 (also works on any mobile network without SIM/credit; EU-wide)
Police 110
Non-urgent medical help 116 117 (ärztlicher Bereitschaftsdienst)
Warnings NINA and Katwarn apps, DWD WarnWetter, Cell Broadcast (DE-Alert, pops up on phones), radio, sirens
Sirens Rising/falling tone for 1 min = Warnung → turn on radio / warning app, follow instructions. Constant tone for 1 min = Entwarnung (all clear).

If networks are down, 112 may not work — then radio and getting to a fire station / police station / Anlaufstelle ("Katastrophenschutz-Leuchtturm", where set up) matter.

Three things:

  1. Listen first. A cheap receiver tells you what's going on.
  2. Higher antenna = more range, more than watts.
  3. Agree on channels and check-in times in advance (card at the end).

2. How radio works (60 seconds)

  • Radios convert voice into radio waves at a frequency (kHz, MHz). Both radios need the same frequency and mode (FM, AM, SSB; for digital the same system).
  • One person talks at a time (PTT = push-to-talk).
  • VHF/UHF (PMR446, Freenet, ham 2 m/70 cm): mostly line of sight — hills and buildings block, repeaters (Relaisfunkstellen) extend range.
  • HF/shortwave (CB 27 MHz, ham HF): bounces off the ionosphere ("skip") and can travel far; depends on time of day and sun.

3. CB radio (CB-Funk) — license-free, vehicle-friendly

  • 27 MHz. FM: 80 channels (the German 40 + 40 extra), AM: 40 channels. Radios must be type-approved for Germany (FM + AM, DE channel set).
  • Power (Germany): FM 4 W, AM 1 W, SSB 12 W PEP. Do not exceed.
  • Key channels: Channel 9 = emergency/help (Notrufkanal, FM and AM); Channel 19 = traffic/highway (Verkehrskanal, FM). Use another free channel for your group.
  • Modes: both radios must use the same mode (FM vs AM vs SSB).
  • Good for: convoys, local coordination, zero setup, no registration.
  • Note: some volunteers monitor Channel 9 but there is no guarantee anyone does. Use it as a last resort, not as a replacement for 112.

4. PMR446 and Freenet — the walkie-talkie families

PMR446 (license-free, EU)

  • 446.0–446.2 MHz, 16 analog channels (plus digital channels on newer radios, dPMR/DMR Tier 1).
  • Max 0.5 W, fixed antenna (no external antenna allowed). Range ~0.5–3 km.
  • Use CTCSS/DCS "privacy codes" only to filter, they do not make a channel private.
  • Cheap, works with every brand (analog). Best default for families and groups.

Freenet (license-free, Germany)

  • 149.0125–149.1125 MHz, 6 channels, max 0.5 W, fixed antenna. VHF often reaches farther outdoors than UHF PMR446. Fewer radios in the field — choose it together with PMR446 only if your group has matching devices.

Not for Germany

FRS/GMRS/MURS are US services — don't buy/use them here (not permitted). Similarly LPD433 has very low power (10 mW): fine for short range, not for emergencies.

5. Amateur radio (Amateurfunk) — the serious emergency option

Amateur radio operators relay messages, coordinate help, and often support authorities (Notfunk, e.g. within DARC / DLRG / THW / Rescue services' support groups). Check local groups (Ortsverband, "OV") now — they know the area's repeaters.

Licensing (Germany, by BNetzA)

Exam on two parts: technology and operations/regulations (Technik / Betriebstechnik & Vorschriften). The question catalogue is public (BNetzA website — download it while you have internet).

Class Typical privileges (summary)
N (Einsteigerklasse, introduced 2024) 10 m, 2 m, 70 cm; up to ~10 W — entry level
E VHF/UHF + parts of HF, up to ~100 W on some VHF/UHF, 10 W on HF
A all amateur bands, up to 750 W

Privileges and power limits are summarized from memory — verify in the current AFuV (Amateurfunkverordnung) / BNetzA tables. Call signs start with DL / DK / DJ / DO / DB… In a life-threatening emergency, calling for help is justified even without a license (see top), but you'll be far more effective if trained.

Main bands

  • 2 m (144–146 MHz, VHF) and 70 cm (430–440 MHz, UHF): local, repeaters.
    • 2 m FM calling frequency (Anruffrequenz): 145.500 MHz
    • 70 cm FM calling frequency: 433.500 MHz
  • HF: 80 m (3.5 MHz, regional at night), 40 m (7 MHz, regional day+night, standard for regional nets), 20 m (14 MHz, long distance by day).

Repeaters (Relais)

Receive on one frequency, re-send on another. Typical shift in Germany: 2 m: −0.6 MHz, 70 cm: −7.6 MHz; many need a CTCSS tone (often 67.0 Hz) or a 1750 Hz tone burst. Print the repeater list for your area (DARC / local OV) now.

Simplex

Talk directly (no repeater) on a simplex channel, e.g. 145.500 MHz. Works when repeaters are down — range is line of sight; go up a hill.

Digital/text (advanced)

Winlink (email over radio), FT8, APRS — need extra gear and training.

6. Broadcast radio — warnings and news (the most important in practice)

  • FM (UKW) and DAB+ are the main channels for official warnings and information. Public broadcasters (ARD state radio, Deutschlandfunk) carry emergency announcements. A battery/crank/solar radio with FM and DAB+ is the single most useful radio item.
  • Medium/long wave (AM) broadcasting is largely switched off in Germany — don't rely on it for German stations.
  • Shortwave (SWL) (≈1.8–30 MHz): hear international broadcasters (BBC, Deutsche Welle, etc.), time signals, ham traffic. No license needed to receive. A receiver with SSB hears much more; a long-wire antenna improves reception.
  • No NOAA weather radio in Germany. Weather warnings: DWD (via apps, FM/DAB+ announcements).
  • Don't listen to BOS/authority or commercial radio (not permitted, § TKG).

7. Antennas, power & range

  • Height beats power. Roof/tree/mast adds more than any amplifier.
  • PMR446/Freenet/CB: limited power and (for PMR/Freenet) fixed antenna by law — stay legal; improve range by position (hills, open ground, outside buildings).
  • Resonant antenna for the band beats random wire (ham).
  • VHF/UHF: range ≈ line of sight. HF: low bands better at night, high bands by day.
  • Power: spare batteries, power banks, 12 V battery, solar panel, car charger.
  • Safety: keep antennas away from power lines; ground equipment against lightning.

8. How to call for help

  1. Listen first for ongoing emergency traffic.
  2. Use the distress call: "MAYDAY, MAYDAY, MAYDAY" (life in danger) or "PAN-PAN" (urgent). In German, "Notruf, Notruf, Notruf" is understood too.
    • CB: Channel 9. Ham: local emergency net, repeater, or 145.500 MHz calling frequency.
  3. Give the essentials slowly, then repeat:
    • Who (name/call sign) — Where (address, street, GPS, landmarks, Rettungspunkt / forest rescue point number) — What (injury, fire…) — What you need.
  4. Spell hard words (alphabets below). Say numbers digit by digit.
  5. Stay on frequency, repeat at intervals if no answer.

German spelling alphabet (DIN 5009)

Anton, Ärger, Berta, Cäsar, Dora, Emil, Friedrich, Gustav, Heinrich, Ida, Julius, Kaufmann, Ludwig, Martha, Nordpol, Otto, Ökonom, Paula, Quelle, Richard, Samuel, Schule, Tanja, Ulrich, Übermut, Viktor, Wilhelm, Xanthippe, Ypsilon, Zacharias. For international contacts use NATO: Alpha Bravo Charlie Delta Echo Foxtrot Golf Hotel India Juliet Kilo Lima Mike November Oscar Papa Quebec Romeo Sierra Tango Uniform Victor Whiskey X-ray Yankee Zulu.
